# Trump Crypto Adviser's 97% Wipeout Shows How Bitcoin Hoarders Are Flailing for a Second Act

**By Bloomberg News**
**August 28, 2026**

David Bailey knows the market is testing him. Over the past 12 months, shares in Nakamoto—his high-profile attempt to build a massive corporate Bitcoin treasury—have plummeted by roughly 97%. 

Yet, despite the bloodbath, the prominent crypto advocate and informal adviser to Donald Trump’s presidential campaign insists he isn't capitulating. 

"I have absolutely zero plan to liquidate Nakamoto. I plan on running this thing for the next 35 years," Bailey told Bloomberg. 

But to survive, Nakamoto is being forced to drastically alter its playbook. The company is now aggressively hunting for cash-flowing businesses to acquire, moving away from a strategy that relied solely on hoarding digital tokens. "We can't control the price of Bitcoin," Bailey admitted. "We can control building useful products and services."

Bailey’s pivot perfectly encapsulates a desperate, sector-wide scramble. Even as Bitcoin surges back to the $80,000 mark—notching its best month in nearly two years—the companies that built their entire identity around holding the asset are being left behind. 

Digital Asset Treasury companies—or "DATs"—are nursing a massive hangover. Over $80 billion has been erased from the market capitalizations of top Bitcoin treasury companies since July 2025. Many of these stocks continue to trade at steep discounts to their Net Asset Value (NAV), signaling a total collapse of market faith in the "DAT 1.0" model of aggressively issuing debt and equity to accumulate Bitcoin.

The irony is striking: the broader crypto market is celebrating, but the digital asset treasuries are on the hunt for anything that will help them start making money again. 

### The Pivot to 'DAT 3.0'

Faced with a model that many analysts now view as doomed, the industry's survivors are frantically pivoting to what insiders call "DAT 3.0." The new strategy? Actually operating a business.

"The market is telling us that there was a misallocation of capital," said Raphael Zagury, CEO of Twenty One Capital, which holds the world's second-largest corporate Bitcoin treasury. "Raising capital and leveraging the capital is okay, but it's a transaction. The way we're looking at this is much more longer-term."

Companies like Twenty One Capital are abandoning the passive treasury model to acquire profitable operating businesses in the Bitcoin ecosystem, drawing comparisons to Warren Buffett’s Berkshire Hathaway. Others are pivoting into physical energy infrastructure for Bitcoin mining, or pursuing traditional financial services licenses to generate fee-based revenue.

"We’re going to eventually see an evolution where we move away from calling them DATs," said Mitty Chang, chief growth officer at Sora Ventures, noting that holding digital assets will just become part of normal treasury operations for revenue-generating businesses.

At the Bitcoin Asia conference this week, the shift in tone was palpable. "We're seeing a clear shift from 'number go up' enthusiasm to a focus on infrastructure, corporate treasury strategies, and regulatory frameworks," noted Brandon Green, CEO of BTC Inc.

### The Hunt for Cash Flow

For Bailey, whose ties to the White House remain strong—he noted he still speaks with the administration regarding their plans for the Bitcoin community—the focus is entirely on M&A. 

After Nakamoto was forced to sell roughly $20 million of its Bitcoin in March at a loss to fund operations, Bailey acknowledges the need for businesses that can "feed a treasury consistently in bull times and bear times." The immediate priority is acquiring scalable companies to generate income, allowing Nakamoto to buy Bitcoin without diluting shareholders or taking on debt.

"We want to be building our Bitcoin balance sheet as much as possible, and we want to be well positioned for the bull market," Bailey said, adding that he hopes to resume buying Bitcoin by the end of 2026. "But cash flow helps you take risk."

As the sector continues to flail for a second act, Nakamoto’s struggles underscore the central tension now facing corporate Bitcoin hoarders: believing the asset will ultimately rise is not the same thing as having the cash flow to survive until it does.
