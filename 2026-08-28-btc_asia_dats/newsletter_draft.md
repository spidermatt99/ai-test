# The Evolution of the Bitcoin Treasury: From Hoarders to Conglomerates

By now, the digital-asset treasury (DAT) bust is well known. The pressing question now is what the survivors are turning into.

The answer, increasingly, looks less like a passive Bitcoin stockpile and more like a strange new breed of conglomerate: part holding company, part lender, part asset manager, part energy play. After months of trading below the value of their crypto, some of the industry’s better-known names are trying to build actual earnings around balance sheets that were once sold largely on the promise of holding more Bitcoin.

When Blockstream CEO Adam Back appeared at Bitcoin Asia last year, his message was simple and prophetic: "Eventually, probably all companies will be Bitcoin treasury companies." He described a straightforward arbitrage where companies would use capital markets to absorb Bitcoin's monetary premium. 

But fast forward to this year's Bitcoin Asia, and the narrative has rapidly evolved. The simple act of holding Bitcoin is no longer enough to appease shareholders, especially as a stubborn bear market has left many of these companies trading at a steep discount to their Net Asset Value (NAV). 

The first generation of DATs proved that public companies could buy Bitcoin. The second showed they could use buoyant equity markets to buy even more. Now comes the harder version: can they turn those balance sheets into businesses that produce cash when the premium is gone and retail investors beat a retreat?

Mitty Chang, chief growth officer at Sora Ventures, calls this new era "DAT version 3.0." 

"Sora's never walked into this with the thesis that you just buy Bitcoin and that's enough," Chang noted. "3.0 is really how do you economically be more productive, and do you have a source of business revenue underneath that? Can you use your Bitcoin on the balance sheet, for example, as credit... or can you create new lines of business from that?" 

Sora is currently raising $1 billion for its third fund to inject capital into these evolving listcos, pivoting away from pure Bitcoin accumulation to funding operating businesses across Asia.

**The Berkshire Hathaway of Bitcoin**

Across the industry, the buzzword has shifted from "accumulation" to "operations."

"I've been very critical of the traditional treasury model for a while," says Raphael Zagury, CEO of Twenty One Capital, which holds the second-largest Bitcoin chest in the world—worth nearly $3.5 billion. He argues that relying purely on market dislocations to buy Bitcoin, a model popularized by early adopters like MicroStrategy, is closer to a hedge fund strategy than a sustainable corporate one. 

"Our approach is going to be different," Zagury explained. "The way we're looking to build our treasury and to give shareholder value in the long term is by finding good operating companies in the Bitcoin ecosystem... Similar, in a sense, to what Berkshire Hathaway did."

Zagury pointed to physical energy infrastructure—generators and high-voltage transformers—as a prime example of the kind of self-financing plays that can generate cash flow, which can then be securitized for cheaper debt. 

**Becoming Bitcoin's Sovereign Wealth Fund**

For David Bailey, whose company Nakamoto also holds significant Bitcoin reserves, the painful lessons of the past year have driven a similar shift in strategy. Nakamoto is actively hunting for M&A targets. 

"We have been thinking kind of from the framework of like, if we were a sovereign wealth fund for the nation of Bitcoin, what businesses could we own?" Bailey said. "It's going to be tough for us to 10x the size of our conference business... So we're looking for other businesses that are scalable, that can help feed income into the business, so that we're constantly able to buy Bitcoin without having to issue shares or take on debt."

This structural shift requires patience—a trait not historically associated with crypto markets. As Zagury noted, transitioning from a transaction-based model of just raising capital to buy Bitcoin, to analyzing, acquiring, and running operating companies "takes time."

But for the survivors of the recent slump, it may be the only path forward. If DAT 1.0 was about proving you could put Bitcoin on the balance sheet, DAT 3.0 is about proving you can build a sustainable, cash-flowing business on top of it.

# DAT 3.0: The End of the Corporate Bitcoin Hoarder

When Blockstream CEO Adam Back took the stage at Bitcoin Asia last year, the prevailing thesis was elegantly simple. "Eventually, probably all companies will be Bitcoin treasury companies," he said, describing the easy arbitrage of using corporate capital to soak up Bitcoin's monetary premium. 

It was a brilliant trade—until the bear market hit. 

Today, the digital-asset treasury (DAT) landscape is littered with listcos trading at a painful discount to their Net Asset Value (NAV). At this year’s Bitcoin Asia, the message was clear: The free ride is over. Simply hoarding Bitcoin on a balance sheet is no longer a viable business model. Welcome to DAT 3.0.

"I've been very critical of the traditional treasury model for a while," said Raphael Zagury, CEO of Twenty One Capital, which commands a nearly $3.5 billion Bitcoin war chest. Zagury argues that the popular playbook—raising capital to buy Bitcoin whenever there's a market dislocation—is effectively a hedge fund strategy, not a corporate one. 

"Raising capital and leveraging the capital is okay, but it's a transaction," Zagury said. To cure the NAV discount, Twenty One Capital is pivoting toward a Berkshire Hathaway model: hunting for fundamentally solid, cash-flowing operating companies—like energy infrastructure—to bring onto the balance sheet. 

**Building the Bitcoin Sovereign Wealth Fund**

The transition from passive treasury to active conglomerate is echoing across the industry's survivors. Instead of relying purely on issuing equity or debt to finance the next Bitcoin purchase, DATs are looking for M&A targets that can generate cash in both bull and bear markets.

David Bailey, whose firm Nakamoto has also felt the sting of the slump, framed it as a structural evolution. "We have been thinking kind of from the framework of like, if we were a sovereign wealth fund for the nation of Bitcoin, what businesses could we own?" 

Bailey is explicitly looking for scalable businesses that can organically "feed income into the business, so that we're constantly able to buy Bitcoin without having to issue shares or take on debt."

**The Return of Fundamentals**

Mitty Chang, chief growth officer at Sora Ventures, coined the shift as "DAT version 3.0." Sora is putting its money where its mouth is, raising a $1 billion fund to help Asia-Pacific listcos expand their underlying businesses. 

"Sora's never walked into this with the thesis that you just buy Bitcoin and that's enough," Chang emphasized at the conference. "3.0 is really how do you economically be more productive... You've got to still have an underlying business." He highlighted portfolio companies like Thailand's Astra, which is aggressively pursuing custodian and asset management licenses to build fee-based revenue streams.

This pivot marks the maturation of the Bitcoin treasury. DAT 1.0 broke the taboo of corporate Bitcoin ownership. DAT 2.0 financialized it through the equity markets. But DAT 3.0 is a return to traditional corporate fundamentals: generating real revenue, making strategic acquisitions, and proving that the company is worth more than the sum of the coins in its cold storage.

# Confessions of a Corporate Hoarder: The 'Buy and Pray' Treasury is Dead 

Not that long ago, buying Bitcoin made you a corporate genius. All a CEO had to do was slap “digital asset treasury” (DAT) on their letterhead, buy a few thousand coins, and watch their stock chart go parabolic.

But what happens when the music stops?

When Blockstream CEO Adam Back painted his corporate utopia at Bitcoin Asia last year, the math sounded inevitable. "Eventually, probably all companies will be Bitcoin treasury companies," he said, suggesting that the arbitrage of buying Bitcoin to absorb its monetary premium was a trade big enough to swallow the cash reserves of Apple and Microsoft for breakfast.

But fast forward to this year's Bitcoin Asia, and the mood has sobered up. The simple act of hoarding Bitcoin is no longer enough to appease shareholders, especially as a stubborn bear market leaves many of these companies trading at a humiliating discount to their Net Asset Value (NAV). The market is punishing the one-trick ponies. 

Enter the era of DAT 3.0.

"I've been very critical of the traditional treasury model for a while," admits Raphael Zagury. As CEO of Twenty One Capital, Zagury oversees the world's second-largest Bitcoin chest—worth nearly $3.5 billion. Growing up in Brazil during the hyperinflation of the 90s, where "money literally losing its value overnight" was a fact of life, he understands the appeal of hard money. But as a Wall Street veteran, he's throwing shade at the MicroStrategy copycats. 

"The way that they're doing is much more akin to how hedge funds allocate capital," Zagury notes, calling the habit of constantly leveraging up to buy the dip a mere "transaction." 

His solution? Build the Berkshire Hathaway of Bitcoin. Instead of just buying coins, Zagury is aggressively hunting for fundamentally solid, cash-flowing operating companies to bring onto the balance sheet. "We will hold infrastructure like generators... high voltage transformers," he says, noting that operating cash flow can be securitized for much cheaper debt. When asked about the painful NAV discount, Zagury is blunt: "The market is telling us that there was a misallocation of capital."

David Bailey, CEO of Nakamoto, is echoing that sentiment. While playing the ultimate long game—"I plan on running this thing for the next 35 years"—Bailey concedes the math has changed. 

"It's going to be tough for us to 10x the size of our conference business," he said. So Nakamoto has pivoted to a relentless M&A hunt. "We have been thinking kind of from the framework of like, if we were a sovereign wealth fund for the nation of Bitcoin, what businesses could we own?" he explained. The goal is to buy scalable businesses that feed the treasury consistently in both bull and bear times, without needing to dilute shareholders to buy more crypto. 

Mitty Chang, chief growth officer at Sora Ventures, has a name for this vibe shift: DAT version 3.0. 

Sora is currently raising a $1 billion fund to supercharge Asia-Pacific listcos, provided they actually *do* something. "Sora's never walked into this with the thesis that you just buy Bitcoin and that's enough," Chang says. "3.0 is really how do you economically be more productive... You've got to still have an underlying business." Case in point: their Thailand-based portfolio company Astra is aggressively hunting for asset management and custodian licenses to build boring—but lucrative—fee-based revenue. 

But not everyone is convinced that pivoting from crypto-hoarder to infrastructure-conglomerate is a guaranteed home run. 

Angela Ang of BitGo offered a much-needed reality check on the conference floor. The real test, she notes, isn't just surviving a bear market by holding, but whether these companies can intelligently "leverage these assets as a financing base instead of a static balance sheet" without blowing themselves up.

And as for buying power plants? "It really depends on what these DATs are diversifying into, and whether their balance sheet is overall strong enough to weather some volatility," Ang warned. "There are some plays, like infrastructure, that are more resilient in market cycles, but also require a mid to long term horizon."

In other words: the easy mode is off. DAT 1.0 broke the taboo of putting Bitcoin on the corporate balance sheet. DAT 2.0 proved you could financialize it. But DAT 3.0 is going to require executives to do something truly radical: run a real business.
