Earlier this month, a closely held Bitcoin investment company [scrapped its planned merger](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-08/cantor-adam-back-s-bstr-seek-new-terms-for-bitcoin-spac-merger "Cantor, Adam Back’s BSTR Seek New Terms for Bitcoin SPAC Merger") with a blank-check company, a combination that would’ve allowed shares in the crypto company to be publicly traded. The two companies — Adam Back’s [BSTR Holdings Inc.](https://bstr.com/ "Bitcoin Standard Treasury Company: BSTR") and an affiliate of Cantor Fitzgerald — said they’d need to revise the terms of their combination “to better reflect current market conditions.”

The market conditions they were alluding to? [A major slum](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-17/bitcoin-slump-why-crypto-token-halved-in-value-since-october-btc-usd " Why This Bitcoin Slump Is Different") [p](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-17/bitcoin-slump-why-crypto-token-halved-in-value-since-october-btc-usd "Why This Bitcoin Slump Is Different") in the valuation of Bitcoin. Since reaching a peak in October, as enthusiasts piled into the digital currency following the return to the White House of crypto-friendly President Donald Trump earlier in the year, Bitcoin has fallen by half. It’s now trading at about $66,000, below its price at the time of Trump’s November 2024 election victory. That’s bad for crypto investors, of course, but also for so-called [crypto treasury companies](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-07-11/crypto-treasury-firms-swarm-wall-street-in-spac-merger-boom "Crypto Treasury Firms Swarm Wall Street in SPAC, Merger Boom"), which raise money in the public markets, then use it to buy Bitcoin or other cryptocurrencies.

Cryptocurrencies are inherently risky investments, prone to dramatic price swings. Most aren’t backed by anything other than the narratives their creators use to entice investors. Crypto treasury companies offer an even wilder ride. Because these companies tap public markets for the financing needed to acquire tokens, their shares tend to behave like a leveraged bet on whatever they’re buying. That means potentially outsize gains, but also steeper losses if their bets don’t work out.

Illustration: James Clapham for Bloomberg Businessweek

As Back, the BSTR co-founder, has put it, companies like his are relying on the idea that Bitcoin’s future value will be high enough that it’s worth the myriad costs of starting and running a publicly traded company whose primary purpose is to buy the cryptocurrency.

In 2025, hundreds of teams had sought to imitate this business model, which was pioneered by Michael Saylor’s [Strategy Inc.](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/MSTR:US "MSTR: Strategy Inc Stock Price Quote - NASDAQ GS"), which has more than $50 billion of Bitcoin holdings. For a few months, it was the hottest trade in crypto. Cosmo Jiang, general partner at Pantera Capital, began studying the idea early last year, and in April 2025 he led a $125 million investment in DeFi Development Corp. — a company that started life as a listed real estate software business and became an accumulator of the cryptocurrency Solana. On April 7 of last year, the shares surged ninefold, setting off a frenzy of copycat launches.

![Michael Saylor](https://assets.bwbx.io/images/users/iqjWHBFdfxIU/iCPrQc8ZM3bU/v1/2000x1334.webp)

Saylor. Photographer: Romain Maurice/Getty Images

At their October peak, crypto treasury companies had amassed more than $120 billion in assets, according to data from Artemis Analytics Inc.

“It got very heated, very quickly,” Jiang says. “We were getting pitched left and right.”

Pantera invested in more than 20 crypto treasury deals and has made money on them thanks to some standout performers, such as [BitMine Immersion Technologies Inc.](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/BMNR:US "BitMine Immersion Technologies Inc Stock Price Quote") and [Hyperliquid Strategies Inc.](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/PURR:US "PURR: Hyperliquid Strategies Inc Stock Price Quote")

But even the standouts are now mostly trading well off their peaks, and investors who came later have been left holding the bag. As a category, crypto treasury companies now control just $75 billion and are sitting on unrealized losses that run into the tens of billions of dollars, Artemis figures show.

Those crypto treasury companies that sought to go public via special purpose acquisition companies, blank-check companies also known as SPACs, are in a particular pickle. Some are only just finalizing deals that were announced a year ago, while others, including Back’s BSTR, are now in peril. A $1 billion vehicle that had lined up former US Commerce Secretary Wilbur Ross for its board was recently [scrapped entirely](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-18/the-crypto-treasury-dream-unravels-after-a-90-stock-plunge), as was a $1.5 billion deal for a company called Ether Machine.

“Current market conditions make it impractical to move forward with the transaction,” Andrew Keys, co-founder of Ether Machine, told investors in an April email seen by Bloomberg News.

For those companies that have listed in recent weeks, the reception has been grim. A crypto buyer called [Avalanche Treasury Corp.](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/AVAT:US "AVAT: Avalanche Treasury Corp Stock Price Quote"), which combined with a SPAC called [Mountain Lake Acquisition Corp.](https://www.bloomberg.com/profile/company/MLAC:US "Mountain Lake Acquisition Corp - Company Profile and News"), is down more than 70% since it began trading on June 11, even as the value of the cryptocurrency it’s focused on has remained essentially flat.

BSTR’s fate will be watched closely by investors in crypto treasury companies. Had its planned deal gone forward, BSTR would have instantly ranked among the largest, and its affiliation with Back further raises the stakes.

![Adam Back](https://assets.bwbx.io/images/users/iqjWHBFdfxIU/ii47FyZFFNVU/v1/2000x1334.webp)

Back. Photographer: Camilo Freedman/Bloomberg

Back, who runs Bitcoin infrastructure company [Blockstream Corp.](https://blockstream.com/ "Blockstream: Bitcoin and digital asset infrastructure"), proposed in 1997 a cryptographic system called Hashcash. Eleven years later, it was cited in the white paper that laid out the framework for what would become Bitcoin. In April, the *New York Times* — citing linguistic similarities, among other findings — [identified Back](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/04/08/business/bitcoin-satoshi-nakamoto-identity-adam-back.html "My Quest to Solve Bitcoin’s Great Mystery: The New York Times") as Satoshi Nakamoto, the pseudonymous Bitcoin inventor whose identity has for nearly two decades remained a mystery. Back denied the claim.

Bitcoin has evolved since its conception, moving beyond the preserve of libertarians and cypherpunks who believed it essential to create a payments system that lay beyond the purview of banks and governments. To them, the very idea of siloing away Bitcoin in corporate coffers would have been anathema. Today, Strategy alone holds about 4% of the total supply, which is hard-capped at 21 million tokens.

It’s now an accepted asset class, with about $72 billion worth of the tokens held by US exchange-traded-fund issuers including [BlackRock Inc.](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/BLK:US "BLK: Blackrock Inc Stock Price Quote - New York") and Fidelity Investments — and an asset class with returns that, for a time, appeared uncorrelated to traditional markets. Bitcoin began to be viewed as a kind of “digital gold,” a narrative that gave Wall Street “a familiar box for something it initially found strange and threatening: a scarce reserve asset, an inflation hedge, something to hold when you don’t fully trust the system,” says Cory Klippsten, chief executive officer of Swan Bitcoin, an investment platform dedicated to the token.

More recently, the [“digital gold” narrative has broken down](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-01-29/crypto-s-digital-gold-myth-exposed-as-traders-pivot-to-metals "Crypto’s ‘Digital Gold’ Myth Exposed as Traders Pivot to Metals"). As old-fashioned gold staged a rally that took it to new highs this January, Bitcoin began to behave more like a traditional risk asset, rising and falling broadly in line with tech stocks. But in October, Bitcoin began diverging from stocks, and the gap has widened ever since.

Crypto treasury companies, meanwhile, have become increasingly complex capital-markets operations. Strategy’s Saylor once told his 5 million social media followers to sell a kidney if they must, but never sell Bitcoin. In May the company sold a nominal sum of the digital currency for the first time in years. More recently, it sold $216 million of Bitcoin.

In August 2025, Back attended a [Hong Kong crypto conference](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-09-02/crypto-s-us-momentum-trump-ties-steal-show-at-hong-kong-event "Crypto’s US Momentum, Trump Ties Steal Show at Hong Kong Event") at which a fist-pumping Eric Trump, one of the president’s sons, predicted that the price of Bitcoin would surpass $1 million. Back was there to talk about Bitcoin treasury companies. The fundamental question facing them is whether they can justify trading at a premium to their net asset value, he told Bloomberg News at the time.

“Effectively the whole thing is an arbitrage on the long-run Bitcoin growth curve being ahead of the cost of capital,” he said. “I think the arbitrage between mispricing of Bitcoin today and its potential future size is so enormous that it can absorb a lot of capital year on year for decades.”

He may yet be proved right in the long run, but the past year has substantially lengthened the odds.

Apple’s Ternus Takes the Reins as CEO, With AI as Job No. 1

![John Ternus&nbsp;and Tim Cook at the Allen &amp; Co. Sun Valley media and technology conference in Idaho&nbsp;on&nbsp;July 7.](https://assets.bwbx.io/images/users/iqjWHBFdfxIU/idYyCqns9L6U/v1/2000x1333.webp)

Stepping into his new role as Apple Inc.’s chief executive officer on Sept. 1, John Ternus won’t have much time before the company begins its annual fall product rollouts. This year the lineup will include an unusually ambitious slate of devices he’s been teasing for months internally, including the first [foldable iPhone](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-26/apple-to-hold-sept-9-foldable-iphone-launch-to-start-ternus-era "Apple to Hold Sept. 9 Foldable iPhone Launch to Start Ternus Era") and a [smart display](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2024-11-12/apple-home-hub-detailed-apple-intelligence-homeos-square-ipad-like-design "Apple’s Next Device Is an AI Wall Tablet for Home Control, Siri and Video Calls") that can recognize who’s speaking and tailor content to them. Ternus spearheaded those devices in his previous job as Apple’s hardware chief, while also overseeing work on products that won’t be ready for this fall, including [AirPods with cameras](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-19/apple-s-camera-equipped-ai-airpods-remain-on-track-for-2027-despite-video-leak?itm_source=record&itm_campaign=Apple&itm_content=Camera-Equipped_AirPods-4 "Apple’s Camera-Equipped AI AirPods Remain on Track for 2027 Despite Video Leak") that can interpret a user’s surroundings, smart glasses, a home security system, a home display attached to a robotic arm and a camera-equipped pendant that can be worn as a necklace or clipped to a shirt. All these devices will be crucial to Apple’s plans to address the most pressing question facing the company: How can it extend its decades-long run of success into the era of artificial intelligence?

Apple is a gadget company at heart, and Ternus has spent 25 years helping perfect its devices, first as an engineer designing Macs. Later he took over iPads, AirPods, iPhones and eventually the entire hardware engineering operation. He’s less experienced at the other aspects of the CEO job, such as finance, sales, legal affairs, government relations and procurement, so he’ll need to rely heavily on Apple’s other executives. Chief Operating Officer Sabih Khan, Chief Financial Officer Kevan Parekh and Eddy Cue, the services chief, are all poised to take on bigger and more influential roles, according to people close to the company. (Like others who spoke to *Bloomberg Businessweek* for this story, they did so on condition of anonymity to discuss Apple’s inner workings.)

Another longtime Apple executive who will retain a key position is Tim Cook. The CEO since 2011, he inherited the company less than two months before the death of Steve Jobs, when Apple was reeling from the loss of its visionary co-founder and there were real questions about its ability to keep growing under anyone else. Yet its revenue roughly quadrupled [during the Cook era](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-04-21/apple-ceo-tim-cook-s-15-year-legacy-by-the-numbers "Apple CEO Tim Cook’s 15-Year Legacy by the Numbers"), to $416 billion, and its market value rose 15-fold. Cook never produced another breakthrough nearing the scale of the iPhone, but he did introduce successful products including AirPods and the Apple Watch. He also developed Apple Pay and Apple Music, turning services revenue from a rounding error into a business that makes more money today than the entire company did when he took over.

Cook’s colleagues threw him a farewell dinner at the Apple Park campus in Cupertino, California, on Aug. 23, featuring hours of tributes from Ternus, Laurene Powell Jobs, other current and former Apple executives, plus a celebratory performance by his favorite rock band, OneRepublic. But he’s not really saying goodbye. Cook will serve as Apple’s executive chairman. He’s told Ternus that he’ll keep managing the company’s relationships with President Donald Trump and the Chinese government, and that he’s eager to handle any other tasks his successor deems necessary. While Cook has talked about plans to take up more outdoor activities and spend time at his vacation home in Palm Springs, he’s also told staffers the company will remain his No. 1 priority, because he “can’t imagine it any other way.”

There’s no indication that any of this reflects a lack of confidence in Ternus, whom Cook began to [seriously consider as a successor](https://www.bloomberg.com/features/2026-apple-next-ceo/ "Apple’s ‘Nice Guy’ Heir Apparent") two years ago. “There is no one on this planet that I trust more to lead Apple into the future than John,” he said in an all-hands meeting with employees after the decision was made public. “He’s an elevator guy. He has the ability to have a clear vision from 30,000 feet, and he has the ability to go to the basement, to go into the deep, precise details of an issue.” Nonetheless, Cook’s not-quite-retirement plan leaves some uncertainty about how quickly Ternus will be able to reshape Apple in his image.

So far, Ternus and Cook have done little to dispel the impression that Ternus represents an extension of the Cook era. When Cook took over for Jobs, he never moved into his office, which remains locked and untouched with the lights off at the company’s old Infinite Loop campus. Ternus, by contrast, is expected to use the office Cook held at Apple Park. The two have been almost inseparable at internal meetings and public events in recent months, and Ternus has taken every public opportunity to praise Cook’s leadership style. At the premiere of the latest season of *Ted Lasso* this summer, the incoming and outgoing CEOs even appeared in virtually identical outfits.

This stage of Apple’s history will naturally fade as the 65-year-old Cook and his deputies — many of whom are also unbelievably wealthy and nearing their mid-60s — begin to leave the company. Ternus’ coronation day is an important moment for the company, but Apple’s changing of the guard will take place gradually. Managing it well will likely determine whether Ternus, 51, ends up being as successful a CEO as his mentor.

Throughout Cook’s tenure, Apple watchers fretted that the company would stagnate if it couldn’t come up with a new hit product whose success could rival the iPhone’s. Although it never quite did, the company fared just fine. AirPods and the Apple Watch served primarily as accessories for iPhones, and Apple’s HomePod smart speaker never captured the public imagination in the same way as competing products from Amazon.com Inc. and others. A decade-long, multibillion-dollar project to build a [self-driving electric car was shut down](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2024-03-06/apple-car-s-crash-design-details-tim-cook-s-indecision-failed-tesla-deal "How Apple Sank About $1 Billion a Year Into a Car It Never Built") in 2024.

At least for now, the prospects look dicey for Apple’s latest big swing: the Vision Pro, a [mixed-reality headset Cook championed](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2023-05-18/apple-s-mixed-reality-headset-may-define-tim-cook-s-legacy "Apple’s New Headset Meets Reality") as a device that could eventually supplant the smartphone and be his crowning achievement. Ternus, who happened to work at a virtual-reality startup before joining Apple, never truly supported the headset, because he felt it was too heavy and expensive to become a mainstream product, according to people who discussed the device with him. In recent months its executives have been trying to persuade him to continue investing in the platform despite its poor commercial performance. Ternus does support, however, Apple’s planned introduction of non-augmented-reality [smart glasses](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-05-31/apple-glasses-late-2027-release-watch-comparison-ios-28-apple-tv-homepod "Apple Seeks to Disrupt the Glasses Market the Way It Did With Watches"), code-named N50, next year. But if head-worn devices don’t gain traction, he’s also open to retreating from the category. This could affect the overhauled Vision Pro, dubbed N224, that’s slated for release as early as the end of 2028.

In June, Paul Meade, Apple’s top executive overseeing its smart-glasses and mixed-reality-headset division, left the company for OpenAI, telling longtime colleagues he was upset about his position in the organization after personnel reshuffling kicked off by Ternus’ elevation. More recently, Apple [cut jobs from the group in charge of the device](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-21/apple-cuts-jobs-in-siri-vision-pro-immersive-video-and-gaming-teams "Apple Cuts Jobs in Siri, Vision Pro Immersive Video and Gaming Teams"). The rare cuts were mostly centered on gaming and immersive video content. But, in a previously unreported move, Apple also laid off Vision Pro employees across the company, including people working on device security, audio engineering, Siri integration, testing and the product’s operating system, which will also power the glasses.

Ternus has pushed back on the often-floated idea that the iPhone is nearing the end of its relevance. He oversaw a three-year revamp of the device, which started with the thinner Air last year, continues with this year’s foldable model and will extend through 2027 with a [glassy model to mark the product’s 20th birthday](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-11/apple-s-glass-centric-20th-anniversary-iphone-remains-on-track-for-2027?itm_source=record&itm_campaign=Apple&itm_content=Glass-Centric_iPhone-5 "Apple’s Glass-Centric 20th-Anniversary iPhone Remains on Track for 2027"). In meetings Apple held this summer to introduce him to Wall Street analysts, Ternus argued that the iPhone will continue to remain central to both Apple’s product strategy and customers’ daily lives, saying he believes people want a do-it-all device with a screen.

Some Apple executives are concerned about Ternus’ ability to navigate difficult logistical issues such as the ongoing [memory shortage](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-01-15/apple-tech-hardware-stocks-face-crisis-as-memory-prices-soar "Apple and Tech Hardware Stocks Face ‘Crisis’ as Memory Prices Soar"), which Apple has publicly blamed for recent price hikes. This is the kind of supply chain risk in which Cook specialized, and he’s been helping Apple deal with it over the past several months. Instead of leading Apple’s response, Ternus is expected to rely heavily on Khan and the company’s procurement, sales and operations teams to fend off component price increases and shortages with as little disruption as possible. The company expects the problem to continue next year.

Another challenge for Ternus will be reversing a recent slowdown in its services business. Apple executives attribute this trend, which they project to continue, to weak game purchases as users shift to TikTok and other forms of entertainment. The company has also had to lower its App Store take rates in many markets because of regulatory pressure, and it’s being forced to allow independent app stores to [operate in the European Union](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-18/apple-lowers-app-store-fees-in-europe-to-settle-dispute-with-eu "Apple Lowers App Store Fees in Europe to Settle Dispute With EU"), which could cause it to lose commissions.

Even closer to home for Ternus, there are signs that Apple’s status as a top destination for hardware talent is eroding. The company, now a mature organization with its share of bureaucratic infighting, has been [losing talent](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-04-21/apple-ceo-ternus-confronts-test-of-retaining-company-s-top-talent "Apple’s New CEO Will Need to Stave Off Exodus of Top Talent") to rivals offering higher pay, more ambitious hardware projects and opportunities to build products from scratch. Hundreds of former Apple employees currently work in OpenAI’s hardware division, which Apple has accused in a [lawsuit of stealing trade secrets](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-10/apple-sues-openai-for-trade-secret-theft-in-blockbuster-case "Apple Sues OpenAI for Trade Secret Theft in Pivotal Case").

Ternus knows retention is a problem. He doled out out-of-cycle bonuses worth hundreds of thousands of dollars to several members of the iPhone design team. He’s also one of the Apple executives who greenlit the lawsuit against OpenAI, in part because its poaching has disrupted several of his hardware teams. At times, Ternus has tried to fend off the departures directly, in one case directing the head of iPhone design, Richard Dinh, to meet face-to-face with an engineer from Singapore, to persuade him not to leave with an offer of a promotion and more money. (The employee went to OpenAI anyway.)

People close to the company say about half the Apple leadership team [could exit in the next few years](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-08-30/john-ternus-management-team-apple-watch-series-12-foldable-iphone-apple-pencil-mtfvmdyu?srnd=undefined). A particularly consequential loss would be Chief Hardware Officer Johny Srouji, who’s taken on Ternus’ old responsibilities and combined them with his chips group. The 61-year-old Srouji last year told Cook he was seriously considering leaving the company, but Cook persuaded him to stay with the expanded role and a request to help with the CEO transition. People close to Srouji say he’s still likely to leave, perhaps in a year or two, once the handoff to Ternus has progressed. The heads of marketing, services and retail have each been at the company for about four decades and could retire in the next few years.

More than anything else, the uncertainty around Apple centers on the way it’s [fallen behind in AI](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2025-05-18/how-apple-intelligence-and-siri-ai-went-so-wrong "Why Apple Still Hasn’t Cracked AI"). Shortly after OpenAI kicked off the generative artificial intelligence boom with the release of ChatGPT in late 2022, Apple undertook a major push into the technology. It built its own large language models, made splashy hires in the field and identified acquisition targets. But the first version of its Apple Intelligence software stumbled, and the company never pulled off a major AI startup takeover. Cook removed Apple’s AI chief and reassigned the executive overseeing Siri, its voice assistant, giving their responsibilities to the company’s software engineering head and the creator of the Vision Pro.

Last year, having failed with its own homegrown technology, Apple began rebuilding the models powering Siri, [paying Google](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-11-05/apple-plans-to-use-1-2-trillion-parameter-google-gemini-model-to-power-new-siri "Apple Nears $1 Billion-a Year Deal to Use Google AI for Siri") to use its Gemini technology as the foundation. This frustrated many members of Apple’s large-language-model team, prompting some, including the group’s chief, to leave for higher-paying jobs elsewhere. At the same time, Apple deprioritized AI research projects, further unsettling its in-house model developers.

A big test will come in September, with Apple’s full-scale deployment of its new [Siri AI](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-03/apple-siri-ai-vs-chatgpt-which-ai-assistant-is-better "Apple vs. OpenAI: How Siri AI Stacks Up Against the New ChatGPT"), which is meant to compete with ChatGPT. Given the high costs of running this kind of AI system, Apple wants to eventually charge for it or figure out a way to make money by integrating third-party apps into the voice assistant. But to get developers or customers to pay, the service will need to overcome Siri’s long-running reputation for not working properly and not being particularly useful. Early reviews of Siri AI have so far been positive.

Ternus has also become interested in using AI to improve Apple’s own product development processes and operations. In one of his final moves as hardware chief, he introduced an AI platform designed to accelerate product development while improving hardware testing and device reliability. “We’re going to be able to solve even harder problems,” he told employees in an all-hands address, arguing that AI could help Apple apply lessons from past products to those still under development.

Aside from using AI to improve the way it builds products, Apple is working to use it to make those products more useful for customers. It’s been planning to release its camera AirPods and flagship home device with a screen for years, but it’s had to delay those releases repeatedly because of shortcomings with the AI software. The new Siri AI is supposed to change that and will also enable new versions of the Apple TV set-top box and HomePod mini. “I’m especially excited to be stepping into this role at this moment, because I am telling you that we’re about to change the world once again,” Ternus told employees at the all-hands. “We have an incredible road map ahead.”

It remains unclear which, if any, AI-powered consumer tech products will catch on. The field has already had some major flops. The most prominent ideas for AI gadgets often involve placing cameras and AI-powered sensors throughout people’s homes and on their bodies. Such plans carry reputational risk for a company such as Apple, which has positioned itself as [more privacy-forward than its competitors](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-07-26/apple-glasses-may-debut-at-wwdc-2027-privacy-camera-features-versus-meta-ms1v7lta "Apple’s Smart Glasses Will Need to Overcome Meta’s Privacy Reputation"). It’s seeking to offset those privacy fears by taking a different tack. Although its smart glasses will include cameras for taking photos and recording video, the cameras in the new AirPods and in a planned home security device won’t be capable of actually recording watchable footage. They’ll instead transmit information to AI systems so they can analyze their surroundings.

Ternus has spent much of his time this year overseeing the company’s industrial- and software-design teams, which have traditionally played a vital role in Apple’s ability to produce distinct consumer products. The teams began reporting directly to him even before the leadership transition became official. The organization he inherits has been decimated by years of departures to former design chief Jony Ive’s new studio and OpenAI. Ternus is making it a priority to rebuild the team’s influence.

As Ternus contemplates the future, he and Cook have been attending planning sessions for 2027 together with essentially every group across the company. Ternus has increasingly taken the lead in these meetings in recent weeks. “I’ll be here to offer my knowledge, experience and be a sounding board anytime I’m called upon,” Cook told employees. Still, he acknowledged, “there can only be one CEO at a time.” As for Apple’s Sept. 9 iPhone event, while Cook may be in the front row, he won’t be featured for the first time in 15 years.

There are numerous examples of how this transition can go wrong. One cautionary comparison is Walt Disney Co., where longtime leader Bob Iger remained as an adviser, clashed with his handpicked successor, Bob Chapek, and ultimately returned as CEO in his early 70s. Marc Benioff of Salesforce Inc. twice brought on co-CEOs who were viewed as potential successors, and both abandoned him. There are also models of success. Amazon’s Jeff Bezos has stayed on as his company’s executive chairman while pursuing multiple interests outside the company, a successful arrangement that’s left Andy Jassy [in control](https://www.bloomberg.com/features/2026-andy-jassy-amazon-ai/ "Andy Jassy Is Rewriting Amazon’s Playbook for the AI Age") of day-to-day operations. Of those relationships, the Bezos-Jassy team is the most similar to Cook-Ternus, say people close to Apple.

One hint about how Cook might gradually wean himself from his duties came via a new job listing at Apple. The company said it was seeking an [executive bodyguard](https://jobs.apple.com/en-us/details/200676699/global-security-senior-manager-executive-protection "Global Security Senior Manager, Executive Protection") who must be able to hike “several miles at a time in austere environments.” Although the job doesn’t mention Cook, the listing is unlikely to be referring to anyone else. He’s spoken about his love of hiking and even mentioned that he plans to do more of it in retirement. Ternus, for his part, is more into cycling and race-car driving. It probably won’t take him long to find that his new day job doesn’t leave him much time for hobbies.
