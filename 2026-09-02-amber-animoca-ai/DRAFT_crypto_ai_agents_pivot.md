# Fading Crypto Stars Chase AI Agents, Minus the Data Centers

*Draft — Bloomberg News — September 2026*

- Amber's Michael Wu, Animoca's Yat Siu bet on agent software after core businesses shrank
- Unlike Bitcoin miners, neither has the hardware that drew billions from AI labs
- Agent "harness" layer is crowded with OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and free open-source rivals

By [REPORTER], with assistance from [ ]

In November 2024, as Bitcoin ripped toward record highs on Donald Trump's election win, Michael Wu sat in a US hotel room and found he couldn't think about crypto at all.

"Amber was a crypto company," the founder of Amber Group said of the trading house he'd built into one of Asia's biggest digital-asset firms. "Crypto finally went marching to new heights. But I was sitting in the hotel room thinking about the little AI agent projects we were building. All I could think about was these things."

Less than two years later, Wu has split his company in two, parted ways with the co-founders who wanted to stay in crypto, and on Sept. 1 relaunched the Nasdaq-listed remnant — Amber International Holding Ltd. — as AMBR, an "agentic AI company." A few blocks away in Hong Kong, Yat Siu, the co-founder and executive chairman of Animoca Brands Corp., is making a similar wager with a personal-agent platform called Minds and a US$10 million fund to seed developers who build on it.

The two men are among the most prominent figures in Asian crypto to attempt what has become the industry's favorite escape hatch: a pivot to artificial intelligence. But their route is different from the one that has worked. The crypto firms that struck gold in AI were Bitcoin miners — companies whose warehouses, power contracts and cooling systems could be repurposed to host the chips that Microsoft Corp., Amazon.com Inc. and Anthropic PBC were desperate to plug in. Amber and Animoca have no such assets. Their bet rests on software: the scaffolding of tools, permissions, memory and workflows — what the industry calls the "harness" — that turns a large language model into an agent that can do a job.

That is a far more crowded field, and one in which the deepest-pocketed competitors are the AI labs themselves.

"The industry defines AI agents as model plus harness. I think that's incomplete," Wu said in an interview on Aug. 31, the day before the relaunch. "I think AI agents are model plus harness plus purpose. Who is the AI agent working for?"

## Businesses That Faded

The pivot comes from necessity as much as conviction. Both companies have watched the businesses that made them famous shrink.

Animoca, which was valued at US$5.9 billion in a 2022 funding round and remains delisted from the Australian Securities Exchange since 2020, reported this week that bookings — its preferred non-IFRS sales measure — fell 45% to US$173 million in 2025 from US$314 million the year before. Its token-advisory arm, which helps crypto projects launch coins and had been its fastest-growing unit, collapsed to US$25 million from US$165 million as the average valuation of the launches it handled dropped about 75%. Cash and stablecoin holdings shrank to US$68 million from US$293 million after the company repaid more than US$160 million of convertible notes issued at the top of the last cycle. Digital assets on the balance sheet fell to US$177 million from US$538 million, and the "off-balance-sheet token reserves" Animoca touts — coins issued by its own projects — were marked at US$306 million, down from US$2.9 billion. Operating expenses of US$175 million exceeded bookings, according to the company's own unaudited figures. Headcount at The Sandbox, its flagship metaverse game, fell 62% to 92.

"2025 was not a great deal," Siu acknowledged in an interview in June, after saying 2024 had been profitable. "It's really been hard for most altcoins." [VERIFY WORDING AGAINST AUDIO]

Amber's decline is visible in a single line of its earnings release. Wealth-management revenue — the fees from running crypto portfolios for family offices and Asia's wealthy — was US$5.3 million in the second quarter, down 54% from a year earlier. Total revenue fell 27% to US$13.9 million. The stock, which traded near US$12 in March 2025 after Amber's crypto wealth unit completed its reverse merger with advertising-technology firm iClick Interactive Asia, closed at US$1.08 on Thursday, for a market value of roughly US$100 million. Shares fell 24% on the day of the AMBR relaunch.

Bitcoin itself trades around US$77,000, down about 40% from its October 2025 peak above US$126,000. Neither Amber nor Animoca has a token-price cycle to ride out anymore; both have decided the next cycle will be about agents.

## The Miners Had Hardware

That is the same conclusion reached by nearly everyone in crypto over the past 18 months. The difference is in what each company brought to the table.

Bitcoin miners became the great beneficiaries of the AI capital-spending boom precisely because they were never really software companies. Core Scientific Inc., which went bankrupt in 2022, signed roughly US$10 billion of data-center contracts with CoreWeave Inc. and has since sold its Bitcoin and is winding down mining altogether. TeraWulf Inc. said in July that Anthropic signed a 20-year lease at its Kentucky campus expected to generate about US$19 billion in revenue. IREN Ltd. struck a US$9.7 billion deal with Microsoft, and Cipher Mining — now renamed Cipher Digital Inc. — landed a US$5.5 billion contract with Amazon Web Services. [VERIFY LATEST DEAL FIGURES]

What those companies sold was land, megawatts and cooling — scarce physical inputs that AI labs could not conjure quickly. Amber and Animoca are selling intelligence-adjacent software into a market where the raw material, the model, is getting cheaper by the month and the harness is increasingly given away for free.

Siu is candid that Animoca's own economics depend on that commoditization. The company recently switched the primary model behind Minds to MiniMax M3, a Chinese model, cutting inference costs by an estimated 90% to 95%, according to a shareholder update in August. "The difference between MiniMax and a frontier model at the 90% utility level is zero," Siu said. "It's a commodity."

That cuts both ways. If the model is a commodity and the harness is open source, what exactly is proprietary?

## A Crowded Layer

The agent harness has gone from obscure engineering concept to one of the hottest layers in technology in less than a year. OpenClaw, an open-source personal agent written by Austrian developer Peter Steinberger, went viral in January and had amassed some 247,000 GitHub stars by March; Steinberger then joined OpenAI. Anthropic's Claude Code processed 7.1 trillion tokens through the OpenRouter gateway in July alone, and Nous Research's open-source Hermes Agent 28.9 trillion, according to figures Animoca cited in its shareholder letter. OpenAI, Anthropic and Google each ship their own agent-building kits, and Chinese giants including Tencent Holdings Ltd. have rolled out OpenClaw-based services.

Animoca's Minds, launched in alpha in February and beta in May, processed 936 billion tokens in July — about 3% of Hermes Agent's volume — and ranked eighth among all applications on OpenRouter, with the No. 3 spot in personal agents, the company said. It is, Animoca said, "already used by thousands of users." The platform's backend is not Animoca's own: it runs on Ethoswarm, a protocol built by portfolio company CryptoSlam.

Wu, for his part, concedes the labs could build what he is building. His argument is that they won't bother.

"The ones who can build Ambre and MIA theoretically, they do not need $50 million. They are the labs or the large internet companies," Wu told analysts on Amber's Sept. 3 earnings call, responding to a question about competition. "They're spending billions or hundreds of billions of dollars winning the model war, not winning the vertical agent war we are fighting."

Amber's two products illustrate the strategy. Ambre, the flagship, is a personal-finance agent that watches a user's brokerage and crypto accounts across platforms and pushes alerts — but does not place trades. "Ambre does not place orders autonomously. Not yet," Wu said. When a user wants to act, they are handed to a human team of former relationship managers. MIA, a marketing agent developed inside iClick, runs campaign operations for more than 100 enterprise customers and, according to the company, cut turnaround times 85%.

Amber also reclassified its revenue. Of the second quarter's US$13.9 million, it labeled US$7.4 million "agentic" — US$3.5 million from an automated market-making system called A-MM and US$3.8 million from marketing and enterprise solutions. The latter is the legacy iClick advertising business, which generated US$4.5 million in the same quarter a year ago under a different label. The company withdrew its financial guidance, saying the old framework no longer fit, and promised a new one at an investor day before year-end.

## Crypto's Case for Itself

Where the two founders diverge from the broader agent industry is in insisting that crypto is not baggage but the point.

"Agents are natural on-chain users," Siu said, arguing that a coming population of tens of billions of agents will need wallets, identities and micropayments that banks cannot provide. "If you don't have money, you don't have true autonomy. Blockchain is the natural machine banking system for an AI agent." He said Minds already pay one another in tokens for skills and compute credits, and in July completed a live pilot with Visa Inc. in which agents identified cardholder rewards and completed purchases.

For Siu, that reframes a decade-old problem. "The challenge, why it may not have played out the way we hoped, is we don't have as many on-chain users as we thought we might have," he said. "And that's why AI is so important."

Wu is more ambivalent. He first pitched a "crypto for AI" thesis in an early 2024 keynote — and recalls an investor joking that he was "very good at making up new narratives." Today he says AMBR is not a crypto company, not a fintech and not a finance company. It contracts with Amazon's Bedrock for model access, fine-tunes open-source models where they beat proprietary ones, and says it doesn't care whether a model is American or Chinese. Crypto remains, in his words, "an add-on service."

## What Could Go Wrong

Analysts and investors who follow the two companies point to three risks. [ADD OUTSIDE VOICES — suggested: agent-infrastructure VC, Web3 equity analyst, ex-Amber executive]

The first is scale. Amber has US$34 million of cash and a US$100 million market capitalization; Wu says the company must become "at least US$100 billion" to be considered successful and that his focus is worth "US$3 billion a month," which is why he is not fundraising. Animoca's cash has fallen 77% in a year, and its plan to list on Nasdaq via a reverse merger with Currenc Group Inc. — announced in November 2025 with a third-quarter 2026 target — passed the end of its exclusivity period on June 30 without a definitive agreement being filed. Siu said in June the company was still working toward a listing in about 12 months. Animoca finished its 2023 audit only in July. [CONFIRM MERGER STATUS WITH BOTH COMPANIES]

The second is that the moat is narrower than advertised. Amber's edge, as Wu describes it, is understanding what wealthy Asian crypto clients want — a niche the company is deliberately walking away from as it targets mass-market users at lower prices. "Do I expect our old business model to be destroyed by our agents? Yes. And I'm okay with that," he said. Animoca's edge is distribution through more than 600 portfolio companies and gaming communities, plus regulatory licenses in Hong Kong and Dubai — assets that matter more for payments than for making an agent smarter.

The third is that every incumbent is building the same thing. Brokerages and exchanges are rolling out their own AI assistants. "All the brokers have their AI chatbots," Wu said. "But they are just trying to get you to have more money on their platform." Whether customers can tell the difference — and will pay for it — is the open question.

Wu, who describes 2024 and 2025 as "the most painful years emotionally as an entrepreneur," including a winding-up petition against the original Amber AI entity in Hong Kong that he attributes to a dispute with a former co-founder, is untroubled by the odds.

"Ninety-five percent of all companies will be gone in five to 10 years," he said. "It's no longer some trees falling and new trees growing. It's a whole continent sinking into the ocean, and a new continent happening."

Whether AMBR and Animoca are on the new continent, or the old one, is what the next 30 months will decide. Wu has done the math: "Every month is US$3 billion in my mind."

Their pitch is that the harness is commoditizing and value sits in "purpose"/distribution. That pitch is plausible but unproven, and the story should keep the skepticism visible.
