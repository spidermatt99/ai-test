The Monetary Authority of Singapore is shifting its scrutiny of the crypto industry toward some of the biggest platforms after years of focusing on lesser-known operations and scams.

The June 26 addition of Hyperliquid, an increasingly popular decentralized derivatives exchange, to the central bank's investor alert list marked the third high-profile crypto firm to land on the list this year. It followed Bybit Fintech Ltd., also last month, and KuCoin in February.

While the list serves as just a warning — it has no regulatory penalties — the additions this year are notable for their size. Before 2024, the list's crypto companies were dominated by fraudulent websites or phishing clones.

Singapore has recently been tightening its oversight of the crypto industry, requiring all companies with operations in the city to be licensed, regardless of whether they provide local services. There is no ambiguity that the companies listed this year could not legally serve Singaporeans, but in the past, firms have taken the hint when landing on the list. Three months after the MAS put Binance on the alert list in 2021, the [company withdrew](bbg://news/stories/R42109T0G1KW) its application for a license in Singapore and left the market.

For the past two years, roughly a quarter of companies added to the list have marketed crypto-related businesses. Yet even among the larger of these companies, few were attracting the kind of business as the three listed this year.

MEXC is the only one listed last year operating at the scale of Bybit, with each having 24-hour cryptocurrency trading volume above $1.5 billion, according to CoinGecko. Bybit and MEXC are also the third- and fourth-largest crypto derivatives exchanges in terms of open interest after Binance and Hyperliquid, CoinGecko data shows.

The Hyperliquid listing came after the network became the subject of speculative frenzy for unlisted stocks. Its perpetual futures contracts, which track everything from commodities such as oil and gold to equities, attracted attention for their offering of SpaceX contracts ahead of the Elon Musk-led aerospace firm's $1.8 trillion initial public offering.

Hyperliquid's inclusion also comes after that of Kyber Network, another decentralized exchange operator that the MAS put on the alert list last year. Proponents of decentralized finance argue protocols are immune to government shutdown, but the companies behind these protocols have increasingly been targeted by regulators.

Singapore's regulatory tightening has pushed crypto firms to [rethink their footprint](bbg://news/stories/SXQPCXT0G1KW) in the city. Bybit, which was founded in Singapore, recently moved its headquarters to Dubai. KuCoin has also changed its headquarters, but had long operated in Singapore after relocating from Hong Kong in 2018.

Hyperliquid has been less public about where it operates. In 2024, it set up a legal entity in Singapore, where it has a registered office in an industrial building five miles north of the central business district.

As with previous cases, the MAS did not provide a specific reason for putting Hyperliquid on the alert list. The regulator said it was based on an assessment of "documentary evidence," and that listed entities "may have been wrongly perceived as being licensed or authorised" to operate in the city.
